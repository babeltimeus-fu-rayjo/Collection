# Collection

Small static web games served from GitHub Pages — browse them at
**https://babeltimeus-fu-rayjo.github.io/Collection/**

Every game shares the same architecture: a pure rules engine (`game.js`), a
networking + UI layer (`app.js`), no build step, and no game server.

## Coup

**Play:** https://babeltimeus-fu-rayjo.github.io/Collection/coup/

An unofficial fan implementation of **Coup** by Rikki Tahta (© Indie Boards & Cards), with its expansion **Coup: Reformation**. 2–10 players; bots fill any empty seat.

- **The game.** Everyone has two face-down character cards — their influence — and 2 coins (the starting player only 1 in a game of two). A turn is one action: Income, Foreign Aid, a Coup for 7 coins (forced at 10), or a character action — the Duke's Tax, the Assassin's assassination for 3 coins, the Captain's Steal, the Ambassador's Exchange — which is a claim, true or not. Any other player may challenge a claim; whoever is wrong turns a card face up, and a card shown to win a challenge is shuffled back into the Court deck for a new one. Once any challenge is settled, an action may be blocked — the Contessa against an assassination, the Captain or the Ambassador against a steal, the Duke against Foreign Aid — and a block is a claim that may be challenged in turn. With both cards face up a player is exiled; the last with influence wins.
- **Reformation.** The lobby adds any of it. *Allegiances:* the starting player picks Loyalist or Reformist and the sides alternate round the table; no one may Coup, Assassinate, Steal from, Examine or block the Foreign Aid of their own side unless everyone left is on one side. *Conversion* pays 1 coin onto the Treasury Reserve to change your own side, 2 to change another player's; *Embezzlement* claims not to hold the Duke and takes the whole Reserve — a challenger says the Embezzler does hold one, and the Embezzler must concede if so, or show their cards (shuffled back for new ones) if not. *The Inquisitor* replaces the Ambassador: Exchange one card, or Examine — another player shows you a card, which you hand back or make them exchange. *Seven to ten players* use four or five of each character. *The two-player variant:* each player chooses one card from a set of the five characters and is dealt the second from a third set.
- **Provenance.** The base rules are the publisher's rulebook, read from [RulesPal's transcription](https://rulespal.com/coup/rulebook) and checked against the [publisher-verified rules on Dized](https://rules.dized.com/game/xzsTtI3VTV-2wvos3otxIg/coup), whose FAQ settles what the rulebook leaves loose: a challenge to an action is resolved before anyone may block it, and the target may still block after a challenge fails; Steal needs a target "who has some coins"; no one sees which cards an Exchange keeps. Reformation's rules are its rulebook, read from [UltraBoardGames' transcription](https://ultraboardgames.com/coup/reformation.php): the components (10 Allegiance cards, the Treasury Reserve, 2 each of the five characters and 5 Inquisitors), the set-up, the three new actions, the Inquisitor variant and the decks for more than six. Judgment calls where the rulebooks are silent, noted in [`coup/game.js`](coup/game.js): the target of an assassination or a steal may answer the challenge question with a block — declared only when the question closes unchallenged, and asked again if a challenge comes first; one block to an action (no second Duke once a block of Foreign Aid has failed); the cards an Embezzler shows to win a challenge are shown to the whole table; Embezzlement may be claimed with the Reserve empty; the Treasury never runs out. The characters' signs and colours are this page's own, not the cards' art.
- **Each player sees only their own.** Every view carries the player's own two cards, every card face up, everyone's coins and Allegiance, and every claim with how it ended; the cards drawn to Exchange and the card shown to an Inquisitor are seen only by the players they belong to, and a target's early block only by the target. Hidden cards travel without their ids, which would give them away. Any player may let other players' claims go without being asked, in the ⚙ drawer — never one aimed at them.
- **Bots.** A bot knows its own cards, the cards face up and the claims made. It weighs a claim by counting — the copies of that character it cannot place, and the unknown cards they could hide among — trusting a claim made twice and never caught a little more and a player once caught a little less, and challenges when a claim looks unlikely and the stakes are its own; always when every copy is accounted for. It blocks with what it holds and bluffs now and then — always the Contessa when an assassination would take its last card anyway. On its turn it Coups at 7 most of the time, assassinates with an Assassin in hand (never a player who claims the Contessa), taxes with a Duke, steals from the rich, Embezzles without a Duke, changes sides when the other side has a Coup or an Assassin pointed its way, and bluffs a character now and then. Bots don't chat.
- **Tests:** `node coup/test-rules.mjs` (102 checks: the deck at every size, the set-up and the two-player coin, every action, challenges won, lost and conceded, the refund of a challenged assassination, blocks and their challenges, both double dangers of assassination, the block after a failed challenge, Steal from a player with one coin and with none, the Exchange, losing influence and exile; Reformation's Allegiances, its targeting and Foreign Aid limits, Conversion, and Embezzlement challenged both ways; the Inquisitor's Exchange, Examine and block; the two-player variant; and that no view shows another's cards) and `node coup/test-play.mjs` (about 1,500 bot games at every size from two to ten, with and without Reformation, the Inquisitor and the two-player variant, every move checked for legality, every card counted and every view checked for leaks).

## Splendor

**Play:** https://babeltimeus-fu-rayjo.github.io/Collection/splendor/

An unofficial fan implementation of **Splendor** by Marc André (© Space Cowboys), with the four modules of its two current expansion boxes, **The Silk Road** and **The Sun Never Sets**. 2–4 players; bots fill any empty seat.

- **The game.** Each turn, exactly one action: take three gems of different colours, take two of one colour from a pile of four or more, reserve a card and take a Gold, or buy a card. Every card bought is a bonus for good — one gem of its colour off every later card — and some carry Prestige points; at the end of a turn a Noble visits whoever has the bonuses on the tile, for 3. No one holds more than ten tokens at the end of a turn. Once someone reaches 15 the round is played out, and the most Prestige wins — on a tie, whoever bought fewer cards.
- **Provenance.** The rules are Space Cowboys' English rulebook (the 10th anniversary edition, which keeps the original's cards and rules), including the setup by table size (7, 5 or 4 tokens of each gem; 5 Gold; one more Noble than players). **Every one of the 90 cards was read off a photograph of the printed card** — crops of two photographs of the original edition's deck, published with [charliebepositive/splendor-original-offline](https://github.com/charliebepositive/splendor-original-offline) — and the list agrees card for card with that project's own transcription and with [monk-time/splendor-fastest-win](https://github.com/monk-time/splendor-fastest-win)'s. Two other public lists ([daniman/splendor-server](https://github.com/daniman/splendor-server), [filipmlynarski/splendor-ai](https://github.com/filipmlynarski/splendor-ai)) each get two level-2 cards wrong; the photographs settle all four. The ten Nobles agree across four independent transcriptions and with a review photograph of the tiles ([Geeky Hobbies](https://www.geekyhobbies.com/splendor-rules/)), which shows six in full and the other four in part. [`test-cards.mjs`](splendor/test-cards.mjs) checks the arithmetic a slip would break: 40/30/20 cards, 8/6/4 of each colour a level, every colour with the same cost shapes at every level, five 4+4 Nobles and five 3+3+3 of neighbouring colours.
- **The expansions.** The host switches modules on in the lobby, alone or together. *The Cities* (The Silk Road): three City tiles, each with a random side up, replace the Nobles; meeting a City's Prestige points and cards at the end of your turn ends the round, and the winner is whoever meets one — the most Prestige among several, then the fewest cards. *The Trading Posts* (The Silk Road): five powers, one tile of each per player, taken at the end of a turn by meeting its demand in cards — a gem after every purchase, a third gem after taking two alike, Gold worth two gems, drawing two and keeping one when reserving from a deck, and a Prestige point per Trading Post. *The Orient* (The Sun Never Sets): thirty cards, two more face up on each row — Gold cards that pay 2 Gold when discarded, copy cards that take the colour of a card you own, double-bonus cards, cards that take another card for free, and cards bought by discarding two of a colour. *The Strongholds* (The Sun Never Sets): every purchase places or moves one of your three Strongholds onto a card, which only you may then buy or reserve, or knocks off a lone rival one; three on one card let you buy it after your action. Each box's extra Noble can join the ten. A purchase can set several things off, and they run in the rulebooks' order before the table is refilled: the copy's colour, the free card, the Trading Post's gem, the Stronghold.
- **The expansions' provenance.** The rules are the two expansion rulebooks on Space Cowboys' site, including their rulings for the modules together (the draw-two Trading Post works on the Orient decks, a Gold card's 2 virtual Gold are each doubled by the Gold Trading Post, a free card is not a purchase and places no Stronghold, Trading Posts and Nobles are kept when a discard takes you below their demand). This is the current edition, not 2017's *Cities of Splendor*, whose Orient cards, some Cities and one Trading Post differ. **The 30 Orient cards, the 7 two-sided City tiles and the two Nobles' names come from Board Game Arena's licensed Splendor**, released with the boxes — the tables its game sends every table; its base deck and ten Nobles agree card for card with the photographed ones above, and every Orient card, City side and Noble that the publisher's product photographs show agrees with it. One City side is provisional: Madrid's second appears in no photograph, and Board Game Arena's tables list it twice, differently; the one used here — 12 Prestige; 3 Diamond, 3 Sapphire, 3 Emerald and 3 Ruby cards — is the entry whose text and cost agree, and it is marked in [`splendor/cards.js`](splendor/cards.js). The Trading Posts' demands and powers are the rulebook's. Two judgment calls: a player may spend a Gold card even when their gems would do, and its virtual Gold goes first where the gems fall short, then in place of the costliest colour; a copy card's colour is chosen by colour, not by card.
- **The table hides almost nothing.** Every view shows the whole table but the decks and the cards reserved blind from the top of one, which only their holder sees — the others see the back and its level. The rulebook has no pass; a player with nothing to take, reserve or buy passes, and the game ends if everyone does.
- **Bots.** A bot tries every legal action on a copy of its own holdings — including the tokens it would then have to give back — and looks one turn further: the best it could do next, as if no one moved in between. It scores the result on Prestige points; bonuses, worth more early and in the colours the table's cards and Nobles want; how near it stands to the best cards it could buy next, counting gems the supply has run out of as slow to come; Nobles', Trading Posts' and Cities' requirements; and tokens in hand. It values the Orient cards' powers, spends Gold cards only when its tokens fall short, and piles its Strongholds onto the card it wants most, toward a conquest — knocking a lone rival Stronghold off that card first. A turn that changes nothing scores worst, so two bots never trade a gem back and forth. Tuned by playing variants against each other, it beats a player who buys the most points it can and otherwise takes random gems nine games in ten. Bots don't chat.
- **Tests:** `node splendor/test-cards.mjs` (the deck and the Nobles, as above), `node splendor/test-rules.mjs` (111 checks on tables set up by hand: the setup at each size, the take rules including fewer than three colours left, two of a kind only from four, reserving with and without Gold and unseen from a deck, paying with bonuses and Gold, the ten-token limit, Nobles by bonuses only and one per turn, the last round, the tie-breaks, and the pass; then every Orient card kind, Gold cards and their lost second Gold, copies and sacrifices with their copies-first rule, free cards, Strongholds placed, moved, knocked off and conquered — with the bought card replaced only after the Conquest — every Trading Post power and the two cards the draw-two Post keeps from the others, the Cities' end and winner, and malformed moves refused) and `node splendor/test-play.mjs` (300 bot games at two, three and four players across all sixteen mixes of the four modules, with every token, card and Stronghold counted after every move and every view checked for leaks).

## BANG!

**Play:** https://babeltimeus-fu-rayjo.github.io/Collection/bang/

An unofficial fan implementation of **BANG!** by Emiliano Sciarra (© dV Giochi), the base game. 4–7 players; bots fill any empty seat.

- **The game.** Everyone is dealt a secret role — the Sheriff's face up; Deputies, Outlaws and a Renegade hidden — and one of the 16 characters, with an ability and life points. On your turn the Dynamite is checked, then the Jail; you draw two, play any cards — only one BANG! — and discard down to your life points. A BANG! hits a player your weapon reaches unless they play a Missed!; distance counts the seats the short way round among players still in, plus a Mustang, minus a Scope. The Outlaws win when the Sheriff falls; the Sheriff and the Deputies when every Outlaw and the Renegade are gone; the Renegade only by being the last one standing.
- **Provenance.** All of it from dV Giochi. The deck is their own card list for the base game: every one of the 80 cards with the suit and value printed in its corner — the ones a "draw!" reads, so they matter (BANG! A♠, 2♦–A♦, 2♣–9♣, Q♥–A♥; Missed! 10♣–A♣, 2♠–8♠; Dynamite 2♥; Barrels Q♠ and K♠; …). The card texts are that list's; the weapons' ranges and the characters' life points are read off the card images on the same site. The rules are the English rulebook, and the rulings its FAQ: Slab the Killer's BANG! needs two Missed! but a lucky Barrel counts as one, and his Gatling needs only one; Jourdonnais with a real Barrel draws twice; Panic! reaches distance 1 whatever the weapon; the exploded Dynamite is discarded before Vulture Sam collects; Bart Cassidy draws for a wound he survives with a Beer, and three for the Dynamite; El Gringo draws nothing from a Duel he started; Suzy Lafayette waits for a Duel to end before she draws; an Outlaw who loses their own Duel rewards no one; whoever played the Indians or the Gatling is the one who eliminated.
- **Each player sees only their own.** The host's browser holds the deck. A player's view carries their own hand and role; everyone's character, life points, cards in play and hand size; the Sheriff's star and the roles of the eliminated — and Kit Carlson's three cards only to Kit Carlson. Where there is no real choice the table makes it for you: a Barrel is always drawn for, Lucky Duke keeps the better of his two cards, and a Beer in hand is drunk when a hit would take your last life point.
- **Bots.** A bot knows its own role and the Sheriff's and reads the rest off what the table does: whoever aims a BANG!, a Duel, Panic!, Cat Balou or the Jail at the Sheriff is marked as an Outlaw, whoever aims them at the marked looks like a friend of the law, and the eliminated show their roles. It also counts which roles are still in play — public, since the deal depends only on the table's size. Outlaws go after the Sheriff and whoever stands by the Sheriff; the law goes after the marked, and a Sheriff with no one marked after the least trusted; the Renegade helps the law while any Outlaw is left, then turns on the Deputies, and on the Sheriff last. Bots heal when hurt (keeping a Beer back for a lethal hit), draw, arm, jail and rob their foes, and shoot the most wanted player in reach. Bots don't chat.
- **Tests:** `node bang/test-cards.mjs` (the 80 cards against the publisher's list, suit by suit and value by value; the weapons' ranges; the characters' life points; the roles at each table size), `node bang/test-rules.mjs` (83 checks on tables set up by hand: distance with Mustangs, Scopes, Paul Regret and Rose Doolan; every card; every character; the FAQ rulings above; the rewards and penalties; every way the game can end) and `node bang/test-play.mjs` (600 bot games at four to seven players, with every move checked for legality, every card counted after every move, and every view checked for leaks; the law wins about two games in five, the Outlaws a little more, the Renegade about one in six).

## Avalon

**Play:** https://babeltimeus-fu-rayjo.github.io/Collection/avalon/

An unofficial fan implementation of **The Resistance: Avalon** by Don Eskridge (© Indie Boards & Cards), with everything in the **Avalon Big Box**. 5–10 players; bots fill any empty seat.

- **The game.** Every player is dealt a secret Character, Good or Evil. Each round the Leader proposes a Team for the next Quest, everyone votes on it at once and the votes turn over together, and an approved Team plays its Quest cards face down — one Fail and the Quest fails, two on the 4th Quest at seven or more. Three failed Quests, or five Teams rejected in a single round, and Evil wins; three successful Quests and the Assassin names Merlin, winning it for Evil if right. With every option off it is the base game.
- **The Big Box.** The lobby adds any of it. *Without Merlin:* "In most cases you will want to play with Merlin, but it is not required" — with no Merlin there is no Assassin and three successful Quests win it for Good, unless the Messengers are in, when the Assassin names both Good Messengers instead. *Characters:* Percival, Morgana, Mordred, Oberon; the Cleric (learns the first Leader's loyalty), the Troublemaker (Good, must lie when checked), the Untrustworthy Servant (knows the Assassin, can be recruited to Evil before the assassination), the Lunatic (must Fail), the Brute (may Fail only the first three Quests), the Revealer (reveals after the second failed Quest), the Trickster (Evil, may lie when checked). *Lancelot,* the two knowing each other or by either variant of switching allegiance. *Modules:* the Rogues (Rogue cards, the Watch token, a Rogue's own victory), the Sorcerers (Magic turns a Quest round), the Messengers (Message cards, backup on the fifth Quest, the Assassin may go after both Good Messengers). *Optional rules:* the Trapper, the Lady of the Lake, Excalibur, Plot cards. Every loyalty check — the Cleric's, the Lady's, the Plot cards' — is a Loyalty card the checked player passes, so the Trickster and the Troublemaker can lie.
- **Provenance.** The rules are the publisher's: the base game's rulebook (a scan of the original, hosted by [Snakes & Lattes](http://upload.snakesandlattes.com/rules/r/ResistanceAvalon.pdf)) and the Big Box rulebook, read from [RulesPal's full transcription](https://rulespal.com/avalon-big-box/rulebook) — the publisher posts no rulebook, and BoardGameGeek's scan is behind a login. The two agree on everything they share: the Set Up chart (3–2 up to 6–4), the Team Building chart, the two-Fail 4th Quest, the reveal script. The Plot deck's make-up is The Resistance's, from its [consolidated rules (v1.1)](https://gamers-hq.de/media/pdf/78/dd/0a/The_Resisance_consolidated_rules_v1-1.pdf) with The Plot Thickens, whose nine cards the Big Box renamed one for one by effect: 2 Are You the One?, 2 Charge!, 1 Show Your Strength, 1 Show Your True Nature, 3 The King Returns, 2 Ambush, 2 Lead to Victory, 1 We Found You!, 1 Restore Your Honor. **Which seven of the fifteen a table of five or six uses is printed on the cards, and no source at hand shows all of them — so Plot cards are offered at seven or more only.** Judgment calls where the rulebook is silent, noted in [`avalon/game.js`](avalon/game.js): Excalibur switches any kind of Success to Fail and any Fail (or Magic) to Success; the optional unseen Evil Sorcerer is hidden like the Evil Rogue (from Evil and from Merlin); Restore Your Honor acts as soon as it is given (the Big Box says the receiver "must take"); without Merlin, Percival (whose "special power is knowledge of Merlin") is not dealt, there is no Assassin unless the Messengers give them someone to name, and so the Untrustworthy Servant without the Messengers, and Mordred, play as a plain Servant and a plain Minion. Designating another agent of Evil to double as the Assassin, which the Big Box also allows, is not offered.
- **Each player sees only their own.** The host's browser holds the deal; each player's view carries their own Character and side, what it lets them see, and the loyalty checks and looks at cards they made themselves — no one else's. Who checks whom is public, but not the Cleric, whose look at the first Leader stays their secret. Votes stay face down until the last is in (a Charge! holder's turns over first); Quest cards are never shown unless played face up, only the number of Fails and the Magic, Rogue and Message tokens. The holder of the Lady may declare what she showed — truthfully or not — and the declaration is recorded at the table. A vote record on the side keeps every Team, its Leader, Excalibur and every vote.
- **Bots.** A bot keeps every possible answer to "who is Evil now?" that fits what it knows for certain — its own Character's reveal, its loyalty checks, Lancelot's switches — and weighs each by how well it explains the table: a Quest with *f* Fails needs at least *f* agents of Evil on it, agents of Evil tend to Fail, to approve Teams with Evil on them and to reject the fifth Team of a round, a Good Lady tells the truth, and with a Trickster or a Troublemaker in the game a loyalty check is strong evidence rather than proof. Good bots propose and approve the cleanest Teams they can see; agents of Evil vote for Teams with their own on them, play one Fail where two would give a partner away, and reject the fifth Team of a round. With the modules they play their special cards, give Excalibur to the player they trust, look with Ambush and set aside with the Trapper where they suspect, hand Plot cards to friends, and watch whoever has been on every Quest that showed a Rogue token. Merlin plays as if he saw nothing three times in ten, and the Assassin names the Good player whose votes and Teams kept clearest of Evil — or, with no Merlin, the two who were on the Quests with the most Good Messages. Bots don't chat.
- **Tests:** `node avalon/test-rules.mjs` (201 checks: both charts, every reveal including the Big Box script, the vote and its ties, the five-rejection loss, the Quest and the two-Fail 4th, the assassination, games without Merlin, the Lady of the Lake, every Character, Lancelot and both variants, the Rogues, the Sorcerers, the Messengers, the Trapper, Excalibur, every Plot card, and that no view shows what it must not) and `node avalon/test-play.mjs` (about 1,900 bot games at every table size across 30 mixes of Characters, modules and rules, with and without Merlin, with every move checked for legality and every view for leaks; Good wins about half).

## Challengers!

**Play:** https://babeltimeus-fu-rayjo.github.io/Collection/challengers/

An unofficial fan implementation of **Challengers!** (2022) and **Challengers! Beach Cup** (2023) by Johannes Krenner & Markus Slawitscheck (© 1 More Time Games, Z-Man Games, Pretzel Games). 1–8 players; bots fill any empty seat, and the Robot takes the spare seat at an odd table.

- **Seven rounds, then a final.** Each round everyone drafts at once — draw five from the Level pile the round offers, keep what the plan says, redraw once, cut anything — and then plays one match against the next opponent on a round-robin schedule. The two players with the most fans (tokens plus the hidden fans on Trophies) play the final. Two players play no final and a lead of 11 fans ends it.
- **A match plays itself.** One card takes the flag; the other side turns cards over until their total equals or beats it; the card that loses the flag goes to its owner's bench with everything under it, one seat per name, six seats. Run out of cards on the attack, or have no seat for a new name, and you lose. The host steps every match of the round at once on a timer, and a card that asks its owner something — Reporter, Sailor, Clairvoyant, Butler, Hacker, Swimmer and the rest — pauses that match until they answer.
- **Both boxes, mixable.** The lobby picks a box (its basic set, starter decks and draft plan) and any five of the twelve additional sets. Beach Cup's Rainbow set counts as every set, *No flag win* and *Special* are in, round one keeps three, and two rounds pay 2 fans for taking the smaller option.
- **Provenance.** The rules follow the publishers' rulebooks: the [first box's](https://cdn.1j1ju.com/medias/33/f3/f9-challengers-rulebook.pdf) in English and Beach Cup's in [French](https://cdn.svc.asmodee.net/production-nextmove/uploads/2024/05/FR_ChallengersBC_RulesBooklet_lowres_compressed-1.pdf), the only language its full booklet is posted in. **Every character is read off pictures of the printed cards** — 141 cards from two community card galleries on TierMaker and the publisher's own samples, and the Newcomer from the Beach Cup rulebook: name, set, level, power, text and the printed *Rare 3x* / *Common 8x* counts, four copies when a card prints none. That gives an arithmetic check the transcription cannot pass by accident — **every one of the twelve additional sets holds exactly 40 cards, and each basic set 20**, as the rulebooks say — and [`test-cards.mjs`](challengers/test-cards.mjs) asserts it, along with each box's printed power limits (A ≤ 3, B ≤ 5, C ≤ 10 in the first box; 4, 7, 11 in Beach Cup). The draft each round is read off the Tournament Plans printed in the rulebooks (the first box: 2A, 2A, 2A or 1B, 2A or 2B, 2B, 2B or 1C, 2C); the Trophy fans are Board Game Arena's (2-2-2-3 in round one up to 9-9-10-10 in round seven).
- **The Robot, at level 1.** At an odd table the Robot takes the spare seat, as the rulebook has it: it does nothing in the Deck Phase and plays its own eight start cards — Alpha 1, Beta 2, Good Bot 3, C.H.A.M.P. 4 and four Cyborgs, whose base power is the round — and "if the Robot wins the match, place the Trophy of the current round back into the box": it is never ranked and never plays the final. A player alone plays it by the two-player rules, where it keeps its Trophies and can win. Its cards are read off two review photographs of them — [GeekDad's](https://geekdad.com/2023/04/assemble-your-team-for-challengers/) (the English edition, all nineteen Robot cards) and [Teilzeithelden's](https://www.teilzeithelden.de/2023/05/23/rezension-challengers-capture-the-flag-all-stars/) (the German edition, the start cards' text boxes in full). Alpha's text box is covered in both; like the other three it is taken to print none.
- **Not in yet, and provisional.** The Robot's harder levels — its eight R cards and three SOLO cards, only partly legible in the photograph — and Beach Cup's sixteen Trainers are not implemented. Bots are full players, who draft, play and score like anyone and can win. The split of the City set's twenty cards between Talent, Dog and Champion copies, and the Beach Cup starter decks beyond the Newcomer and the new Dog, are provisional and marked in [`challengers/cards.js`](challengers/cards.js). The printed Tournament Plans' exact pairings are replaced by a round-robin with the same effect: someone new every round while there is someone new.
- **Bots.** *Quick* drafts by instinct. *Steady* plays a hundred and sixty practice matches in its head for every candidate pick and every cut — a match takes about 0.02 ms to simulate — against benchmark decks drafted at random from the sets in play, never anyone's real deck, drawing from its own shuffled copy of the piles. It won 70–80% of two-player tournaments against Quick and took the championship in eight or nine of ten four-player ones with two of each. In a match every bot choice follows simple local rules: strongest card on top, weakest underneath, clear a seat that lends nothing, never free a seat on the other bench.
- **Tests:** `node challengers/test-cards.mjs` (the arithmetic above), `node challengers/test-effects.mjs` (each rule and each kind of effect on stacked decks: equal takes the flag, same names share a seat, the seventh name loses, immediate bonuses kept and attack bonuses dropped in flag possession, flag loss before flag gain, the Cowboy, the Yeti, the Troll, the Dwarf, the Zeppelin, No flag win, forced choices answering themselves, the Deck Phase's picks and single redraw), `node challengers/test-play.mjs` (192 bot tournaments across both boxes and mixed sets at every count from one to eight, with a census after every step that no card is ever lost or duplicated, and the Robot's rules checked after each: it never drafts, keeps its eight cards, and at a table of three or more never holds a Trophy, is never ranked and never plays the final) and `node challengers/test-bot.mjs` (that the bot's choice cannot see anyone else's deck and leaves the piles untouched, and that Steady beats Quick).

## Toy Battle

**Play:** https://babeltimeus-fu-rayjo.github.io/Collection/toybattle/

An unofficial fan implementation of **Toy Battle** by Paolo Mori & Alessandro Zucchini (© Repos Production). Exactly 2 players; the host can fill the second seat with a bot.

- Each turn is one action: **draw 2 Troops** onto your rack (cap 8), or **place 1 Troop** and apply its effect, then its base's effect if that base is special.
- You may place on an empty base, one of your own, an enemy Troop of **strictly lower** strength, or the enemy H.Q. — and every placement must sit at the end of a continuous path from your own H.Q. running only through bases **you** occupy.
- Win by capturing the enemy H.Q., by reaching the Terrain's Medals objective, or — if a player can neither draw nor place — on Medals, with **ties going against whoever ran dry**.
- **Provenance, and it is uneven.** The rules, all eight Troops (Kwak the joker, Skully 1, Cap'n 2, Jumbo 3, Hook 4, XB-42 5, Star 6, Roxy 7) with their effects and printed Notes, and all eight Terrain powers are transcribed from the publisher's English [rulebook](https://cdn.svc.asmodee.net/production-rprod/storage/games/toy_battle/rules/toy-en01-rules-1744030094a4kQo.pdf) and [player aid](https://cdn.svc.asmodee.net/production-rprod/storage/games/toy_battle/helpsheet/toy-en01-player-aid-mkt-1750679915Pm3l4.pdf). **All eight board layouts are transcribed from photographs of the physical boards**, taken and marked up by their owner, since Repos publishes no straight-down scan of them. Each Terrain in [`toybattle/game.js`](toybattle/game.js) records its `source` and the lobby shows it; every one reads from the board, and every detail has been checked against the board by its owner — paths, Medals, special bases, objectives and Tropical Pool's value triangles.

  Two patterns held on every board: **a region ringed by n bases holds n − 2 Medals**, an H.Q. on the ring not counted, and **the objective is half the board's Medals, rounded down**. `test-effects.mjs` enforces both, which makes the Medal counts a free check on the paths: a missed or invented path changes how many bases ring a region. The board is drawn turned so each player sees their own H.Q. at the bottom, whichever way the printed board puts it.

- **Boards are written as data** at the top of `game.js`: nodes tagged `hq0`/`hq1`/`special`/`only:…`, a list of path segments, and regions given as the nodes that bound them. Each board keeps the frame it was copied in off its photograph (`frame`, and which way up its `card` lies), and the parser turns every one into millimetres on the real 40 × 24 cm card — the frames did not share the card's proportions, and percentages had squashed Castle Field and Tropical Pool to three fifths of their height. The real boards needed all of that — regions ringed by three to six bases, regions walled by a moat rather than a path, two H.Q. a side, value limits on individual H.Q. — and the parser rejects a path to a node that does not exist or a region whose nodes are not even joined.
- **Drawn to be read.** The printed tiles are about 3 cm on paths of 6 to 10, which reads across a table and not on a phone, so the screen draws its bases half again as big relative to the paths — and [`toybattle/layout.js`](toybattle/layout.js) then nudges only what would crowd: every base at least 24px clear of every other, every path 14px clear of any base it does not reach, and nothing more than 26px from where the card prints it (most move under 8). Medals go wherever their region has the most open ground. On a landscape screen the board takes the full height with the scoreboard and your rack beside it.
- **Reading the table.** Hover anything — a Troop in your hand or on the board, a special base, an H.Q., a count — and it explains itself after a tenth of a second; on a phone, tap anything that is not a move. The Troop you pick up shows its card above the prompt. One scoreboard puts **Medals, Hand and Reserve** in columns, a row per player. For practice the host can **show the bot's hand** — the 👁 beside its name, or *Show the bot's hand* under ⚙ Testing; it is only ever a real bot's rack, never a disconnected player's that a bot is covering.
- **The bot searches.** It runs alpha-beta over the real engine with iterative deepening, and it is not allowed to cheat: the opponent's rack is hidden and both reserves are shuffled, so before searching it throws away everything it is not entitled to know and deals a plausible hand from what is public — its own rack, the stacks on the board (which the rules let anyone inspect), the discard, and how many Troops the opponent holds. Pointing at a Troop for the Battlefield sniper is blind by the rules, so it generates one move standing for all of them. Four skill levels are picked in the lobby; each carries a wall-clock ceiling as well as a node budget, so the same level plays the same way on a laptop and a phone and cannot freeze the tab.
- **What the search is worth, measured.** Every rung of the ladder beats the one below it over 100 games with seats swapped each game — Steady beats Quick 66%, Sharp beats Steady 59%, Ruthless beats Sharp 58%, and Ruthless beats Quick **78%**. The ladder stops at Ruthless because that is where it stopped separating: a search four times bigger than Ruthless scored 48% against it, so the extra thinking was buying nothing and the levels were rescaled down to where the difference is real. Ruthless costs about 25ms a move.
- **The sharper signal is blunders** — turns that leave your own H.Q. open to an immediate capture when another legal move would not have. The one-ply bot does that on about 0.16% of its turns; the searching bot runs at 0.0–0.4%, and the test fails if it ever exceeds 3%.
- **What did not work**, all measured and all reverted: weighting a region corner by the strength of the Troop holding it (46% over 200 games), averaging two or three determinized deals instead of searching one of them deeper (45–52%), and late-move pruning (51% over 400 games). Depth is what pays here, up to a point; the rest was complexity for nothing.
- **Tests:** `node toybattle/test-layout.mjs` (every board on the card, and drawn with no base touching another, no path under a base it does not reach, nothing far from its printed spot, and every Medal on open ground in its own region), `node toybattle/test-bot.mjs` (that the bot cannot see through the opponent's rack — the same position is put to it twice under a pinned RNG with the hidden rack swapped, and the answer has to match — plus that it takes an open H.Q., does not hang its own, and beats the shallow bot), `node toybattle/test-play.mjs` (tile conservation, Medal bookkeeping and turn passing over 320 bot games, one check per action) and `node toybattle/test-effects.mjs` (each rule by hand, then a sweep confirming every Troop and every Terrain power actually fires in play).

## dnup

**Play:** https://babeltimeus-fu-rayjo.github.io/Collection/dnup/

An unofficial fan implementation of **dnup** by Kei Kajino & Gilles-Romain Fonteny (© Asmodee Group), following the [official rules](https://www.dnup.game/rules/rules_en.pdf). 2–5 players; the host can fill empty seats with bots.

- Every card has an **active value** (upright) and an **inactive value** (upside down). Anything you take from the table is rotated 180° as it enters your hand — *that's a dnup*.
- All cards are dealt out (8 each). On your turn you discard the set in front of you, then do exactly one action: **play a set** (must beat any same-size set on the table, which bounces back to its owner rotated), **add one matching card** to an opponent's set, **take an opponent's set** (rotated), or **rotate your whole hand**.
- First player out scores **+2** (their last set lingers one lap), second out scores **+1** and ends the round. First to **4 points** wins. Two players use the official duel variant: two play areas each, two consecutive turns, first to **2 round wins**.
- **Deck:** the `DECK_*` arrays in [`dnup/game.js`](dnup/game.js) are the real 40-card manifest transcribed from the physical deck — 26 base cards plus setup groups of 4 (3+ players), 6 (4+), and 4 (5). All cards are always dealt, so hands are 13 / 10 / 9 / 8 at 2 / 3 / 4 / 5 players. High values are scarce by design: three 10s and five 9s in the whole deck.
- **Bots:** the host adds/removes bot players in the lobby (🤖). Bots run in the host's browser and choose among all four actions with simple lookahead heuristics (`botChoose`).

## Love Letter

**Play:** https://babeltimeus-fu-rayjo.github.io/Collection/loveletter/

An unofficial fan implementation of **Love Letter** by Seiji Kanai (© Z-Man Games / Asmodee), using the Premium edition's expanded cast. 2–8 players, bots included.

- Hold one card, draw one, play one, and use its effect to deduce what everyone else holds. Last player standing wins the round — or the highest card when the deck runs out.
- The full Premium cast is implemented: Assassin and Jester (0), Guard (1), Priest and Cardinal (2), Baron and Baroness (3), Handmaid and Sycophant (4), Prince and Count (5), King and Constable (6), Countess and Dowager Queen (7), Princess (8) and Bishop (9) — including the Assassin's counter-strike, the Sycophant's forced targeting, the Bishop's guess-and-redraw, and the Count's showdown bonus.
- **Deck note:** the deck grows with the table — the 16-card classic core at 2–4 players, 24 at 5–6, and the full 32 at 7–8. The 32-card composition matches the Premium box; how the intermediate tiers are trimmed is a reconstruction, kept as plain data at the top of [`loveletter/game.js`](loveletter/game.js).
- Token targets follow the printed rules: 6 to win at 2 players, 5 at 3, 4 at 4, 3 at 5+.

### How multiplayer works

No game server. The site is static (GitHub Pages) and play is peer-to-peer:

- **WebRTC data channels** carry all game traffic directly between browsers.
- **STUN servers** (Google's public ones, `stun.l.google.com:19302` etc.) are used for NAT traversal, configured in [`dnup/app.js`](dnup/app.js) (`RTC_CONFIG`).
- **Signaling** (how peers first find each other) uses the free [PeerJS](https://peerjs.com) cloud broker, because static hosting can't run a signaling server. Only handshake metadata passes through it.
- Topology is a **host-authoritative star**: the room creator's browser owns the game state, validates every move, and sends each player a personalized view (you never receive other players' hands). If the host closes the tab, the room ends.
- Players behind very strict/symmetric NATs may fail to connect with STUN alone; add a TURN server entry to `RTC_CONFIG` if you need one.

### Develop locally

Any static file server works:

```bash
python3 -m http.server 4179
```

then open `http://localhost:4179/dnup/`. Multiplayer works between two tabs.

Both games are fuzz-tested at the engine level (random-legal and all-bot playouts across every player count, checking card conservation, termination, and rules invariants).

Code layout: [`dnup/game.js`](dnup/game.js) is the pure rules engine (deck manifest, legality helpers shared with clients, bot brain); [`dnup/app.js`](dnup/app.js) is networking (PeerJS) + UI; no build step, no dependencies beyond the PeerJS CDN script. Engine invariants are fuzz-tested (random-legal and all-bot playouts across every player count).
